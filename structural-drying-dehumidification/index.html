Drying is a measured job, not a waiting game

Once standing water is gone, the building still holds a lot of it. Framing, subfloor, drywall and insulation take on moisture slowly and release it slowly. Structural drying is the controlled phase that pulls that water out using airflow, warm air and dehumidifiers, while readings show whether it is working.

This page covers the drying phase only. The first days of stopping and removing water are described under water mitigation. If water is still on the floor, call (385) 988-9410 first and read this afterward.

Safety steps while drying equipment runs

Drying equipment is simple, but it changes how a room behaves. These habits keep the process safe and the readings honest.

Do these in order

  1. Keep cords and hoses clear. Equipment cords cross floors and hallways, so keep children and pets out of the work area.
  2. Leave the equipment alone. Do not move, unplug or switch off air movers and dehumidifiers to save noise, because readings depend on steady operation.
  3. Keep doors and windows as set. Opening a window to help drying usually lets humid air back in and slows the room down.
  4. Watch for wet outlets. If a receptacle, switch or light fixture sat in water, leave it off until an electrician has checked it.
  5. Report new leaks at once. A fresh drip or a damp spot that spreads means the source is not fixed, so call (385) 988-9410.

Fix the source first

Drying cannot finish while water keeps arriving. A plumber, roofer or HVAC technician handles the leak itself, and we start or resume drying once it is stopped.

The equipment and what each piece does

A drying setup is chosen from readings, room size and the materials involved.

Air movers

Low, fast fans that skim air across wet surfaces so water evaporates from them. They are placed along walls and across floors, not aimed at the middle of a room.

Refrigerant (LGR) dehumidifiers

They chill incoming air to condense vapor into water that drains away. They work well in warm, closed rooms with a lot of moisture to remove.

Desiccant dehumidifiers

They use a drying wheel instead of cold coils, so they keep working in cool rooms and garages. Winter jobs in unheated spaces often call for one.

Ports, mats and containment

Small ports let air into closed wall cavities, mats pull moisture from floors, and plastic sheeting holds the drying zone together so equipment does not dry the whole house.

Daily monitoring visits and the dry standard

On each visit, a technician records temperature, relative humidity and moisture content at the same marked spots. Air readings show whether dehumidifiers are keeping up. Material readings show whether wood, drywall and concrete are actually giving up water.

The target is a dry standard taken from similar unaffected materials in your own home, such as an interior wall that never got wet. A wet wall is dry when it reads close to that reference, not when it simply feels dry. Our process follows the published ANSI/IICRC S500 reference for water damage restoration.

Why dry outdoor air does not dry a closed room

A cold, dry day outside can still hold very little water. Warming that air indoors helps, but opening windows lets the room's humidity swing with the weather and gives no control over how fast materials dry. Even when outdoor air is dry, an unheated room stays cool, and cool materials release water slowly.

A closed, contained room with equipment lets the dehumidifier hold the air at a low, steady humidity so the wet materials keep releasing water. That is why windows stay shut while equipment runs.

Do it yourself or call

Household fans and a store-bought dehumidifier can help with a small, clean spill on a hard surface. They do not reach wet insulation, wall cavities or subfloor, and they cannot tell you when a structure is dry.

If water soaked carpet pad, drywall or flooring, or if anything sat wet for more than a day or two, call (385) 988-9410. We can read the structure, explain the plan and tell you plainly if drying is not the right step. Compare options on hardwood floor water damage if wood floors are involved.

What affects the cost of structural drying

Scope and price are explained before work starts. These factors move the plan more than anything else.

  • How many rooms and how much square footage are wet
  • Which materials hold the water, such as concrete, framing or hardwood
  • How long the area sat wet before drying began
  • How many pieces of equipment the readings call for and how many days they run
  • Whether walls, floors or ceilings need ports, mats or containment

Insurance often responds differently to sudden events than to slow leaks, and flood damage is usually handled separately from plumbing failures. Photograph the damage, call your insurer early and keep your drying logs. We provide documentation of readings and equipment, but coverage decisions are the insurer's.

When equipment comes out, and what it does to your power bill

Equipment is pulled only after the final readings match the dry standard. Pulling it early is how damp framing ends up behind finished walls, so a short extra day usually costs less than a second job.

Dehumidifiers and air movers do add to electric use while they run. Ask for the number of units and running days at the start, so you know what to expect, and tell your insurer about the added power use.
