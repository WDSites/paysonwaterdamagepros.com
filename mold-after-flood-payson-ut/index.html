Mold After a Flood in Payson: How Fast It Grows

By the Payson Water Damage Pros team, 2026

EPA guidance says to dry wet materials within 24 to 48 hours. After that, the risk of mold climbs with every day things stay wet.

How fast mold can start after a flood

Mold needs moisture, so the clock starts when materials get wet and stay wet. EPA guidance says to dry wet materials within 24 to 48 hours to prevent growth. After that window, wet drywall, carpet pad and insulation become likely places for it to take hold.

That is a risk, not a certainty. Fast extraction, removal of materials that cannot dry and steady dehumidification can keep it from starting. The practical goal is to get water out and the structure dry before the window closes.

Why floodwater makes it harder

Floodwater from outside is usually Category 2 or 3 under the ANSI/IICRC S500 standard, because it picks up soil, runoff and sewage on the way in. Contaminated water soaks into porous materials and cannot be cleaned out of them, so those materials generally come out instead of being dried in place.

Clean Category 1 water, such as a supply line break, gives you more room to save materials. Floodwater does not. That is why a flooded basement is a different job from a leak under a sink.

What stays and what must go

This is the usual outcome after a flood, with exceptions based on how long things stayed wet.

What to do while you wait for help

These steps protect the structure and your claim.

Before the crew arrives

  1. Stay safe first. Stay out of water near outlets or the panel. Call 911 or the utility for electrical or gas risk.
  2. Document everything. Photograph and video the water line, damage and ruined items before anything is moved.
  3. Lift what is dry. Move dry belongings to a clean room. Leave soaked porous items for the crew.
  4. Ventilate if it is safe. Open windows only if outdoor air is drier and the area is safe. Don't run fans across contaminated water.
  5. Keep records. Keep a call log and receipts for temporary repairs, as the Utah Insurance Department's consumer guidance advises.
  6. Call a crew. Call (385) 988-9410 so extraction and drying can start inside the window.

Drying versus remediation: which one do you need

If everything was caught early and materials dry to their reference readings, simple drying is enough. That is the goal of flood damage restoration. If visible growth or a musty smell shows up in materials that stayed wet, remediation may be needed.

Remediation follows the ANSI/IICRC S520 standard for professional mold remediation. It involves containment, removal of affected materials and cleaning with HEPA air scrubbers, and the water source must be fixed first. Testing and clearance are separate decisions, and you can ask the crew how they handle them. EPA guidance says homeowners can often handle growth under about 10 square feet, but larger or floodwater-related jobs call for professionals. Our mold remediation page covers it in depth.

Dry out after a flood, then check irrigation connections before the season starts.

What to document before anything is thrown out

Photograph the water line on each wall, the depth markings on furniture and every item that will be discarded. Write down what each item was and roughly when you bought it. A crew adds moisture readings, a map of wet areas and an itemized scope.

Keep a few small samples of ruined flooring or drywall if your insurer asks for them, and ask before you haul everything to the curb.

Flood coverage is separate

Most homeowners policies exclude flood, per FEMA FloodSmart, and NFIP flood policies generally take 30 days to take effect. If you only added coverage recently, ask your insurer when it began. We help document damage with photos, moisture readings and an itemized scope of work.

A lender sometimes requires mold to be cleared before a sale. If that applies to you, keep drying logs and ask the remediation company what documentation they provide.
