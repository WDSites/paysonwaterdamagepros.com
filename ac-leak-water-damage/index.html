Most AC leaks are small, but they can go unnoticed

An air conditioner that leaks is almost never a flood. The usual picture is a slow drip or seep that wets a closet floor, a section of ceiling drywall or the insulation around the unit. The risk is not volume. It is that nobody sees it for weeks.

That is why a small AC leak can still need real drying. Wet drywall, baseboard and subfloor stay damp long after the surface looks fine, and a drip you ignore in July can leave a stained ceiling or soft floor by September.

First-hour safety steps for an AC leak

These steps limit the water and keep the electrical risk down. Stop at any step that feels unsafe.

Do these in order

  1. Turn the unit off. Switch the system off at the thermostat, and at the breaker if you can reach it without stepping in water. A running unit keeps making condensation.
  2. Stay clear of wet electrical. Air handlers have wiring and controls. Do not touch the unit or nearby outlets if there is standing water around them.
  3. Stop the water supply. If the leak is from an evaporative cooler, close its water supply valve.
  4. Soak up what you can. Use towels or a wet vac for the pan overflow and move stored items off the floor.
  5. Photograph the area. Take photos of the unit, the pan and any stains before cleanup.
  6. Call the right trades. An HVAC technician fixes the leak. Call (385) 988-9410 if drywall, insulation or flooring got wet.

Do not run a leaking unit

A unit that keeps cycling can keep adding water to a pan that is already full or a drain that is blocked. Leave it off until the HVAC technician has looked at it.

Where the water comes from

Cooling a house pulls moisture out of the air, and that water has to leave through the condensate drain line. Algae and debris can clog the line, and the water then backs up into the pan under the air handler. If the pan overflows or cracks, it drips onto whatever is below.

Frozen evaporator coils are another source. Poor airflow or a refrigerant problem can ice the coil, and when the unit shuts off the ice melts faster than the pan can handle. Air handlers in closets, attics and utility rooms are especially likely to wet drywall, since a drip there lands on finished material.

Evaporative coolers work differently

Some homes use an evaporative cooler, often called a swamp cooler, instead of or alongside refrigerated air. These units are fed by a water supply line and use a float, pump and wet pads. A stuck float, a split line or a rusted pan can leak onto a roof, a wall or a floor.

The repair belongs to an HVAC technician or plumber. Water damage from the line often shows at the wall or ceiling near the unit. We dry the structure once the supply is shut and the leak is addressed.

How we dry after an AC leak

Our process follows ANSI/IICRC S500, a published reference for water damage restoration. It is a reference we use, not a credential.

Wet vacs and extraction

Wet vacs pull standing water from closet floors, pans and carpet before it wicks further into baseboard and subfloor.

Moisture meters and thermal camera

Meters and a thermal camera show how far a slow leak has spread through drywall, insulation and flooring.

Targeted material removal

Soaked insulation and weakened drywall are cut back to dry material. We keep the removal as small as the readings allow.

Air movers and dehumidifiers

Air movers and low-grain refrigerant dehumidifiers dry the remaining materials. Readings are checked until they reach a dry level.

Fix it yourself or call someone

Clearing a clogged condensate line is something some homeowners do, but it is HVAC maintenance, and an HVAC technician can also find the reason it clogged. Mopping up a small pan overflow is fine. Anything wet inside a wall, ceiling or subfloor needs a moisture check.

If the leak was small and quickly dried, you may need nothing more. When it sat for days, our water damage cleanup page explains how we handle the wet materials, and the rebuild is described under water damage repair.

What affects the cost of AC leak water damage repair

Scope and price are explained before work starts. Most AC jobs are small, but these factors set the size.

  • How long the leak ran before it was found
  • Whether the water reached drywall, insulation or only the floor
  • How much material can be dried in place rather than removed
  • The location of the unit, such as a closet, attic or utility room
  • How many days of drying equipment and monitoring are needed

Insurers often treat sudden, accidental water differently from gradual seepage, and many AC leaks are slow. Document the damage, check your policy for exclusions and call your insurer early. We provide moisture readings and photos, but we cannot promise coverage.
