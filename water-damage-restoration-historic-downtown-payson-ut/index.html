Local note: Historic Downtown Payson

What the National Register paperwork says about this area

The Payson Historic District is listed on the National Register of Historic Places. Its nomination describes a cruciform area roughly bounded by 500 North, 300 East, 500 South and 400 West, and it includes the earliest developed portion of the Payson town plat from 1866. The period of significance runs from 1857 to 1957.

The nomination notes settlement-era to mid-century architecture, a commercial core and a concentration of bungalows. The Peteetneet School, separately listed, is reported just outside the district's east edge. Those are descriptions of the district, not findings about any one building, so homes differ and it is fair to ask what yours contains.

Source type: National Park Service National Register of Historic Places nomination for the Payson Historic District and a Wikipedia summary for the Peteetneet School location

Safety steps before anyone touches a wet wall or ceiling

Water in an occupied building can be live, contaminated or heavy overhead. Slow down and keep people clear first.

Do these in order

  1. Stay out of standing water. Keep people and pets out of water that may be electrically live or contaminated, especially if it touches outlets, cords or appliances.
  2. Leave the breakers alone. Do not use breakers or electrical equipment while standing in water. If you smell gas or see sparks, call the utility or 911 for immediate danger.
  3. Shut off water only if safe. If the supply valve is reachable and the floor around it is dry, close it. Otherwise leave it and call.
  4. Treat sewage as a professional job. Sewage or contaminated floodwater should not be handled with fans, household vacuums or do-it-yourself cleanup.
  5. Photograph when it is safe. Pictures of visible damage and the affected rooms help you describe the loss on the phone and to your insurer.

Unsure whether it is safe?

If you cannot tell whether water is near live wiring, leave the area and call the utility or 911 for immediate danger.

A shop below and an apartment above is a different job

A wet ceiling in a shop with an apartment above is not the same problem as one in a house. More than one household may be affected, the source may sit in someone else's space, and the lower floor may need to keep working while it dries.

Ask early who can open the unit above, where the shared shutoff is and who else should be told. Sort out access before the crew arrives.

Older buildings, newer repairs: ask what is behind the wall

Buildings from the settlement era through mid-century often warrant a check of what is behind the wall once it is opened. Homes differ, and nothing about the district tells us what any single wall holds. Homeowners should ask the crew what they find and what it means for the scope.

Before repairs, ask Payson City Development Services (Building Division) whether a permit or review applies. Active drying is covered under emergency water removal and structural drying, and if walls stayed wet, ask about mold remediation before they are closed.

What affects the cost of water damage work here

Every job is scoped on its own. These factors move the scope most.

  • How much of the building is wet and how far the water traveled
  • Whether the water was clean or contaminated
  • What the floors, walls and ceilings are made of, and what is found once they are opened
  • How many spaces or households are affected in a mixed building

Photos can help document visible damage, moisture readings can support a restoration record, and an itemized scope can describe the work. Check your policy and talk to your insurer, because coverage depends on the cause and the policy terms.
